---
title: The Law of Ed's Sheds
date: 2026-08-14
---

There's a lot of doom and gloom out there about AI and redundancy. I get it - our jobs are changing very quickly, and it feels like personal agency is being diminished. It's very painful to learn that the hard-earned skills that you trained for and practiced for years are no longer in demand as they were before.

Those of us who work in the software industry are feeling it first, but it's clear that we won't be the last. The so-called "intelligence revolution" is sweeping through white-collar work with a vengeance and for most of us the choice is to hang on or jump off.

I want to inject some optimism into the discussion. I call it the law of "Ed's Sheds".

My father Ed was of the generation that lived before the age of cheap mass production. So he preferred to keep everything for possible future reuse. In other words, like me, he was a hoarder - he just had a better excuse.

As he retired to live further from the city with a bigger garden, he solved his storage issues by getting a bigger shed. Sure enough, that one filled up, and he added another, and another. Oddly enough, no matter how many sheds or 40' containers that went into that garden, they just seemed to fill up.

Dad did eventually stop adding sheds (after the sixth!) but it does give me my personal favourite mnemonic - something to remember him by, if you will.

## The law of Ed's Sheds
Available capacity will _always_ get used.

You can apply this law in a number of ways. As [Parkinson's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson%27s_law), it's why many businesses will "never waste a crisis" and indulge in periodic redundancies when they know they can get away with it - companies only stay lean if they are hawkish on capacity.

## What does this mean for AI?

AI represents a massive increase in capacity for information processing. Our ability to process, reason about and manipulate data is advancing so much, and so quickly, that some have predicted that all problems will be "solved" within a few years, which means that in a well balanced society, people will never need to work hard again. 

There will be no IT jobs, lawyers, or even manufacturing work because AI, combined with robotics, will make us all redundant. Cue dramatic predictions of societal collapse, or emergence of a utopia.

We've heard this before. [Computers were meant to make office work obsolete.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paperless_office) Some assumed that Moore's law meant that there would be no need for new computers after 10 years of doubled capacity. [The internet would make communication so ubiquitous that universal understanding would soon dawn.](https://www.eff.org/cyberspace-independence) Many speculated that Google translate spelled doom for language education.

I'm afraid that Ed's sheds would disagree. We'll find new ways to use all of this extra capacity. Some will be remarkable. Some will be dangerous. All will be exciting. Most will create new jobs. Those jobs are different to what I spent the first 25 years of my working life doing, but hey, that's OK - ride or die, right?

## So what should I do?
Which brings us to the practical part. How do we adapt? I can only answer for my own experience in software development, engineering and architecture.

It's not that complex - be the person directing the AI, not the person replaced by it. 

Learn what AI is good at and what it's bad at. Understand the difference between creativity, reasoning and judgement - and how they can usefully intersect.

Learn the basics of prompting - how to set up structured decision records, readable requirements, agent execution pipelines, guardrails. 

Learn how to delegate to the agent for longer and longer periods at once, while still controlling costs, quality and retaining human judgement. 

If your organization does not buy tickets for this particular ride, use a free or cheap subscription to your model of choice to learn outside working hours. There's a good chance you'll need a new job in a few years. Ride or die applies to companies too.

It's different, sure, but all the best engineers I've known are both curious and lazy - this is just another way of getting more work done, more quickly.

This is a big change, but it's not the end. There's a yawning space in this brand new shed - figure out how to fill it!
